## **The Philosophic Turn for AI Agents**

### **Exordium**

Philipp Koralus’s essay makes a bold and imaginative proposal: that the future of artificial intelligence ought to be modelled on philosophy rather than rhetoric. Against the backdrop of “nudging” and the manipulation of choice architectures, he calls for AI systems that preserve autonomy by guiding us toward _erotetic equilibrium_—a state in which our judgements remain stable when tested against the central questions of a problem-space. His proposal deserves both admiration and scrutiny.

### **Points of Agreement**

Koralus is right to identify the dangers of AI as a new rhetoric. Contemporary recommender systems and large-scale choice architectures already function less as instruments of truth-seeking than as engines of persuasion. Optimised for engagement, they risk transforming us into passive subjects of a technological order.

He is also right to insist on the importance of decentralisation. Truth-seeking is communal, adaptive, and contestable; it flourishes in conditions of plurality. His notion of “inquiry complexes”—the evolving sets of questions a community takes to be central—is a powerful way of naming the tacit background against which reasoning unfolds.

Most importantly, I am sympathetic to his claim that philosophy aims at wisdom rather than mere rhetoric. At its best, philosophy cultivates the orientation that allows us to live responsibly in a world too vast for our cognitive reach.

### **Wisdom and Orientation**

Yet wisdom here requires a richer description than Koralus provides. The end we seek is not only truth but _orientation_: a framework wherein the world shows up as significant, a _home_ within the vast web of relations that give our lives meaning. Each of us carries an implicit picture of “the world,” yet our “context window” is narrow. Few of us "‘own’ our stance on quantum mechanics, the correspondence theory of truth, or the deepest ethical disputes. Our worldviews are pre-installed by nature, education, and tradition.

Philosophy promises, at its best, to allow us to revisit and sometimes re-code or re-engineer these inherited _defaults_. Yet autonomy here is fragile. The sceptic cannot live their scepticism; as Hume taught, nature always rescues us. Autonomy is therefore never absolute, but partial and precarious.

It is here that Koralus’s examples, such as “It is raining,” strike me as trivial, even comical. The real questions of wisdom concern governance, justice, metaphysics, time, consciousness. If AI is to contribute to philosophy, it must help us orient ourselves amid these immensities, not in trivial perceptual cases.

The problem grows sharper when we ask: what is wisdom, and why do we prize it? Colloquially, we call someone wise not for having instant answers but for having lived through uncertainty, failure, and risk. Wisdom comes from having “gone through,” from having endured situations where outcomes could have gone either way. A wise musician, entrepreneur, or scientist has suffered, failed, and learned. By contrast, large language models provide fluent answers without ever undergoing risk. Nothing is at stake for them. They are, in John Haugeland’s [phrase](https://www.amazon.com/Giving-Damn-Essays-Dialogue-Haugeland/dp/0262035243), systems that cannot “give a damn.”

The danger is that wisdom becomes trivialised. Just as AI art impresses without having skin in the game, so too AI dialogue risks offering the _appearance_ of wisdom without the substance. The struggle is erased; the journey is outsourced.

This is why I agree with Koralus that the model of the philosophical tutor is apt. The best nonfiction authors already embody it: they map a contested domain, show the rival positions, articulate assumptions, steelman each side, and then disclose their own judgement. The reader gains not an answer but orientation. Sellars and Brandom [describe](https://www.amazon.com/Space-Reasons-Selected-Wilfrid-Sellars/dp/0674024982) this as inhabiting the “space of reasons”: philosophy as building a home within a vast web of commitments and inferences. This, rather than instant answers, is what genuine philosophical AI should scaffold.

But to achieve this, we must be precise about the nature of AI systems. Koralus is not. He invokes Socrates but glosses over what that example demands.

### **Technē, Socratic Limits, and Automation**

In Plato’s dialogues, Socrates repeatedly turns to _technē_—the crafts of shoemakers, builders, smiths—as analogies for knowledge. A craft is algorithmic: it transforms inputs into outputs through repeatable steps. Wisdom in _technē_ lies in knowing the most efficient sequence and teaching it to others.

Socrates’s brilliance is in applying this model of _technē_ to justice, knowledge, and the good. The result is failure. Computation does not exhaust the human world. The dialectical method lays bare the limits of technical procedure in the face of ethical and metaphysical questions.

This has direct relevance for AI. Large language models are precisely instruments of _technē_. They replicate forms, sequence tokens, and generate outputs. But they cannot inhabit the dialectic. They canot own commitments, face risks, or answer challenges. Plato already worried that writing itself outsourced memory and trivialised thought; how much more true of automated systems that mimic inquiry without responsibility.

Here we touch the philosophical heart of the matter: _automation, however powerful, cannot replace the dialectical struggle_. Like Aristotle’s later logic, it codifies and outsources. But Socratic _aporia_ reminds us that human finitude, not _technē_, is the ground of philosophy.

### **The Central Difficulty: “AI” as a Floating Signifier**

Here we arrive at my central objection. Koralus speaks of “AI agents” as though they were a single, coherent class. But there is no such thing as AI _simpliciter_. There are rule-based systems, reinforcement learners, generative models, retrieval-augmented systems, and hybrids. The ethical and epistemic challenges vary dramatically across them.

Large language models, in particular, are trained to predict tokens, not to reason dialectically. Their learning is statistical, not philosophical. To turn them into Socratic tutors requires additional architecture: fine-tuning, scaffolding, external retrieval, and interpretability layers. Without such details, Koralus’s proposal risks remaining metaphorical rather than practical.

### **Objections in Full**

1.  **Trivialisation.** By focusing on examples like “It is raining,” Koralus underplays the gravity of philosophical orientation. AI’s role, if it is to be philosophical, must address contested domains of ethics, metaphysics, and governance, not trivial perceptual cases.
    
2.  **Scale.** The analogy to a philosophy tutor is persuasive at small scale. It is feasible to design a fine-tuned LLM that scaffolds inquiry in this way. But it is unrealistic to imagine massive platforms like OpenAI, Anthropic, or Gemini designing their systems as philosopher-kings. Their scale and incentives point elsewhere.
    
3.  **History.** Philosophy itself is not a story of convergence but of persistent disagreement, shaped by rhetoric and power as much as reason. Likewise, AI research has advanced not purely through dialectic but through strategic manoeuvring. To present the problem as if inquiry alone could guide outcomes is naïve.
    
4.  **Limits of Autonomy.** Most of our worldviews are inherited; ownership is partial at best. The idea that we could all become philosophers through erotetic design risks romanticism. In practice, autonomy remains fragile and mediated by tradition.
    
5.  **Technē vs Wisdom.** The Socratic lesson is that technical procedure always comes up wanting when applied to the good, the just, or the true. Large language models are powerful technai, but they are not dialecticians. To confuse fluency with wisdom is to miss the point of Socratic aporia.
    

### **Conclusion**

I’m a fan of Koralus’s project. His book _Reason & Inquiry_ is fantastic and warrants multiple reads. Likewise, his essay deserves respect for articulating a vision of AI as philosophical tutor rather than digital rhetorician. His emphasis on decentralisation, inquiry complexes, and autonomy-preserving design are admirable. Yet his treatment of AI is insufficiently precise. Wisdom cannot be automated wholesale. Socrates’s lesson about the limits of _technē_remains urgent: automation can assist inquiry, but it cannot replace the dialectical struggle through which wisdom emerges.

The challenge now is to translate his philosophical ideal into engineering design. That means not treating “AI” as a monolith but recognising the specificities of large language models, alignment methods, and system architecture. Only then can the aspiration of erotetic equilibrium be transformed from metaphor into practice.

Now, go read his essay and find your home in the space of reasons.
